\begin{abstract}
Retail analytics usually stops at prediction: it reports forecast accuracy but
never shows that acting on the model would have earned money. This paper
describes an end-to-end decision system built on 524{,}926 store--week--product
records from the dunnhumby \emph{Breakfast at the Frat} panel, covering 55
products across 77 stores over 156 weeks. Own-price elasticity is estimated with a
Hausman instrument and empirical-Bayes shrinkage, promotion uplift with two-way
fixed-effects difference-in-differences, and demand with a smeared
gradient-boosted model; these feed constrained pricing, markdown scheduling by
backward induction, and forecast-driven replenishment. Every estimator is
validated on the real panel and on a schema-identical synthetic replica whose
elasticity and promotional lift are known by construction, where the naive
promotion estimate is biased by $0.557$ in log-lift against $0.010$ for
difference-in-differences. On the real data the
share of products with economically impossible positive elasticity falls from
$5.5\%$ to $0\%$, the forecast improves on the strongest naive baseline by
$31.0\%$ in WAPE, markdown scheduling recovers $6.6\%$ more cash than a fixed
discount ladder, and forecast-driven replenishment cuts inventory cost by
$48.1\%$ while raising fill rate. Each result is published with a grade
recording what was actually observed. Under that rule a modelled pricing gain of
$21.0\%$ is kept apart from an observational check on the same decision that
returns $+1.6\%$ and is inconclusive ($t=1.0$); before promoted weeks were
excluded that check reported $+741\%$ at $t=129.5$, entirely an artefact of
comparing full-price weeks against clearance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A supermarket chain decides every week, for every product in every store, what
price to charge, whether to promote, how deep to discount ageing stock and how
much to reorder. The decisions interact: a promotion changes demand, that changes
the reorder quantity, and the discount driving it eats the margin the reorder was
meant to earn. Most work here stops at prediction --- fit a demand model, report
an accuracy figure, finish. But knowing that units rise when price falls does not
say whether price should fall, and the quantities such systems rely on are biased
in a known direction: retailers cut prices in weeks when they already expect
strong demand and promote harder in stores that are already busy. On the panel
used here a plain log--log regression returns a \emph{positive} own-price
elasticity for $5.5\%$ of products, and a naive before-and-after comparison
overstates an in-store display by more than a factor of two.

This paper describes a system built to avoid that failure. The contributions
are:

\begin{itemize}
\item an elasticity estimator combining store fixed effects, a Hausman
      instrument and empirical-Bayes shrinkage, which removes every
      economically impossible estimate from the panel;
\item a promotion-uplift model separating the merchandising mechanic from the
      price cut that accompanies it, with a parallel-trends placebo test;
\item three decision modules --- constrained pricing, markdown by backward
      induction and forecast-driven replenishment --- each benchmarked against
      the incumbent heuristic, and inventory against a perfect-foresight bound;
\item a validation design running every estimator on a schema-identical
      synthetic replica with known parameters;
\item an evidence-graded ledger labelling every headline result by what was
      actually observed, which publishes the disagreement between the modelled
      and observational estimates of the same pricing decision.
\end{itemize}

The fourth makes the rest credible: on real data the true elasticity is
unobservable, so internal consistency proves nothing, while identical code on
data whose parameters were fixed in advance turns it into a measurement. The
fifth decides what may then be claimed.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Forecasting, elasticity and endogeneity}
Gradient-boosted trees remain a strong default for the tabular, count-valued
series retail produces \cite{friedman2001}, and recent work couples forecasting
directly to inventory decisions \cite{yang2025}, the arrangement adopted here.
That observed prices are endogenous to demand is long established; Nevo's
analysis of ready-to-eat cereal \cite{nevo2001} is directly relevant, both
methodologically and because cereal is one of our four categories. The standard
remedy is an instrument correlated with cost but not local demand, canonically
the same product's price in other markets, with strength judged by the
first-stage $F$ against a threshold of ten \cite{stock2005}. Where per-product
estimates are noisy, empirical-Bayes shrinkage reduces total error
\cite{efron1973}.

\subsection{Promotion effect measurement}
Difference-in-differences with two-way fixed effects is the standard way to
separate what a promotion caused from what merely coincided with it
\cite{angrist2009}, resting on a parallel-trends assumption that should be tested
rather than assumed \cite{bertrand2004}; causal methods are increasingly used for
marketing targeting \cite{lemmens2025}.

\subsection{Pricing, markdown and inventory decisions}
The profit-maximising price under constant-elasticity demand follows the Lerner
condition \cite{lerner1934}, while clearance with fixed stock and a deadline is
solved classically by dynamic programming \cite{gallego1994}. For replenishment,
deep reinforcement learning is competitive rather than dominant against
established heuristics \cite{gijsbrechts2022}, motivating exact dynamic
programming where the transition model is known. Unconstrained algorithmic pricing
raises margins in ways consistent with weakened competition \cite{assad2024}, and
agents can reach supra-competitive prices without explicit coordination
\cite{fish2024}; both argue for hard guardrails. Explainability requirements are
surveyed in \cite{debock2024}.

\subsection{Summary and research gap}
Validating causal estimators on synthetic data with known parameters is
established practice rather than something we introduce: Schultz \emph{et al.} do
so before turning to real data \cite{schultz2024}, and Poinsot \emph{et al.} argue
such experiments are necessary for causal machine learning to be trusted
\cite{poinsot2025}. We do not claim that design as new. What we did not find is a
retail pipeline in which the causal estimates feed every downstream decision, all
decisions are scored on one shared held-out split against the heuristic each would
replace, and every result carries a grade recording what was actually observed ---
including where the modelled and observational estimates of the same decision
disagree.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
Let $q_{ist}$ be units of product $i$ sold in store $s$ in week $t$ at shelf
price $p_{ist}$ against reference price $b_{ist}$, with indicators $F_{ist}$
(mailer), $D_{ist}$ (display), $R_{ist}$ (temporary price reduction) and unit cost
$c_i$. The objective is gross profit $q(p-c)$, except in clearance where it is
cash recovered, the stock being already bought (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).

\subsection{Dataset and preprocessing}
The dunnhumby \emph{Breakfast at the Frat} panel \cite{dunnhumby} covers 156
weeks from January 2009 to December 2011 across cold cereal, frozen pizza, bag
snacks and oral hygiene. It is unusual among public retail datasets in reporting
shelf and reference price separately, making discount depth directly observable,
and in carrying three merchandising flags rather than one.

Loading normalises the workbook to a fixed column contract and repairs
recoverable defects: 24 unusable rows were dropped, 6{,}232 whose reference price
fell below shelf price were reset, and two implying a discount deeper than $90\%$
were clipped. What remains is $524{,}926$ store--week--product records over 77
stores and 55 products, of which $28.5\%$ sit under some promotion at a mean
discount of $20.3\%$, with a median within-product price standard deviation of
$0.379$. Unit cost is absent, as no retailer publishes
it, so it is derived from a per-category gross-margin assumption and every
dependent conclusion is recomputed across a $\pm 5$ percentage-point band. Splits
are strictly temporal --- weeks 1--110 train ($368{,}410$ rows), 111--130
validation ($70{,}010$), 131--156 held out ($86{,}506$) for one final evaluation
--- since a random split would let later weeks inform earlier ones.

\subsection{Elasticity estimation}
Demand is constant-elasticity, so in logs $\varepsilon_i$ is the coefficient on
log relative price. Three specifications are estimated per product: $\log q$ on
$\log(p/b)$; the same plus store fixed effects, merchandising indicators, two
seasonal harmonics and a trend; and a third instrumenting own price with the
leave-one-out mean log price of the same product in \emph{other} stores that
week,
\begin{equation}
z_{ist}=\frac{1}{|S|-1}\sum_{s'\neq s}\log p_{is't}.
\label{eq:iv}
\end{equation}
Prices elsewhere move with shared supplier and commodity cost shocks but not with
the demand shock local to store $s$. Two-stage least squares is applied within the
fixed-effects transformation and reported only where the first-stage $F$ exceeds
ten. Estimates are then shrunk toward their category value,
\begin{equation}
\tilde\varepsilon_i = w_i\,\hat\varepsilon_i + (1-w_i)\,\bar\varepsilon_{c(i)},
\qquad
w_i=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma_i^2},
\label{eq:shrink}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_i$ the standard error of $\hat\varepsilon_i$ and $\tau^2$ the
between-product variance within the category, estimated robustly from the median
absolute deviation. Abundant data gives small $\sigma_i$, so $w_i\to 1$ and the
estimate is left alone.

\subsection{Causal promotion uplift}
The lift attributable to each merchandising mechanic is estimated by
difference-in-differences with two-way fixed effects,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\log q_{ist} = \alpha_{is} + \delta_t
 &+ \beta_F F_{ist} + \beta_D D_{ist} + \beta_R R_{ist}\\
 &+ \gamma \log\!\left(p_{ist}/b_{ist}\right) + u_{ist},
\end{split}
\label{eq:did}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{is}$ absorbs everything permanent about a product in a store and
$\delta_t$ everything common to a week, so stores not running a display act as
controls for those that are. The price term is essential: a display is normally
accompanied by a discount, so without $\gamma$ the display coefficient would
absorb the price effect and the mechanics would not be comparable. Validity is
tested with a placebo, shifting each indicator two weeks earlier within its
store--product series; a substantial coefficient on an event that has not yet
happened would signal pre-existing divergence.

\subsection{Demand forecasting}
Weekly units are forecast with gradient-boosted trees on lagged demand, rolling
means, seasonal harmonics, relative price and the merchandising flags. Next week's
price and promotion calendar are legitimate features, since retailers commit to
them in advance. The model is fitted on $\log(1+q)$, as retail demand is
multiplicative and right-skewed. Exponentiating a log-scale fit under-predicts,
since the expectation of a convex transform exceeds the transform of the
expectation; the uncorrected model showed a $-5.1\%$ bias, removed by a Duan
smearing factor \cite{duan1983}. Accuracy is reported as weighted absolute
percentage error, which unlike MAPE is not dominated by slow movers. A second,
counterfactual forecast sets promotion flags to zero and price to reference; that
baseline is what the pricing and markdown modules optimise around, so promotional
inflation is not mistaken for organic demand.

\subsection{Constrained price optimisation}
\label{sec:pricing}
Differentiating $q(p)(p-c)$ under constant-elasticity demand gives the Lerner
condition \cite{lerner1934},
\begin{equation}
p^{\star} = c\,\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon},
\qquad \varepsilon < -1,
\label{eq:lerner}
\end{equation}
equivalently that the optimal gross margin is $1/|\varepsilon|$.

For an inelastic product, $-1<\varepsilon<0$, the profit function has no turning
point and the optimum is unbounded --- not a defect of the derivation but a sign
that single-product profit is the wrong objective. Four guardrails are imposed: a
cap on any single price move, a floor on discount depth, a minimum gross margin,
and a band bounded by prices the chain already charges elsewhere, the last
limiting drift toward supra-competitive pricing \cite{assad2024,fish2024}.

\subsection{Markdown scheduling}
Clearance has fixed stock and a deadline, so it is solved exactly by backward
induction over states $(t,I)$ of week and remaining inventory,
\begin{equation}
V_t(I)=\max_{d}\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\min(D_d,I)\,p_d + V_{t+1}\!\left(I-\min(D_d,I)\right)\right],
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
with terminal condition $V_T(I)=I\cdot v$ for salvage value $v$, and demand
$D_d\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_d)$ where $\mu_d$ combines the baseline forecast with
the elasticity response to discount $d$. The objective is cash recovered, not
margin: the stock is already bought, so its cost is sunk. Subtracting unit cost from each sale makes salvage look better than
discounting whenever the discounted margin falls below salvage value, and the
model then never marks down at all. Backward induction is preferred to
reinforcement learning because the transition law is known, so it returns the
exact optimum rather than an approximation.

\subsection{Forecast-driven replenishment}
The classical order-up-to level over lead time $L$ is
$S=\mu(L+1)+z\sigma\sqrt{L+1}$, with $z$ from the newsvendor critical fractile. It
assumes normal, stationary demand, and neither holds: demand is right-skewed, and
next week's demand depends on next week's promotion, which is already known, so a
static policy carries promotional safety stock all year. Ours rebuilds the target
each week from the forecast, sizing safety stock on \emph{forecast error} rather
than total demand variability,
\begin{equation}
S_t=\sum_{k=t}^{t+L}\hat q_k + z\,\hat\sigma^{\text{res}}_t\sqrt{L+1}.
\label{eq:invpolicy}
\end{equation}
The residual spread $\hat\sigma^{\text{res}}_t$ uses an expanding window over
weeks already elapsed, since a buyer in the first week cannot know how accurate
the forecast will later prove.

\subsection{Validation design}
A synthetic replica with identical schema gives each product a known elasticity
and each mechanic a known log-lift ($0.38$ mailer, $0.55$ display, $0.22$
temporary price reduction), with promotion assignment deliberately confounded
with season and store tier so the problem is not made artificially easy. The
pipeline runs unchanged on both.

%=============================================================================
\subsection{Explanation and evidence grading}
Two components sit after the decision modules. The first explains a
recommendation: for the forecast we use SHAP values \cite{lundberg2017}, which
approximate what a tree ensemble responds to, while for price no approximation is
needed, since the Lerner condition is a closed form and can be reported as its own
arithmetic --- elasticity used, assumed cost, unconstrained optimum, and the
constraint that bound. The same pass records how often each guardrail binds and
whether increases fall disproportionately on value-tier stores. The second grades
evidence, labelling each result by what was actually observed:
\textsc{measured} when prediction and outcome are both in the data,
\textsc{replayed} when a policy is re-run against demand that really happened,
\textsc{observational} when outcomes are real but treatment was not randomised,
and \textsc{modelled} when the number depends on the estimated demand curve and
was never charged. A modelled result is given as a range over the assumptions it
rests on, never as a single figure.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Elasticity recovery}
Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}(a) shows estimates against known values: naive least squares
sits systematically below the diagonal, overstating price sensitivity, while the
instrumented and shrunk estimator lies on it. Table~\ref{tab:elast} gives a naive
bias of $-1.923$ and mean absolute error falling from $1.923$ to $0.089$, a factor
of $21$, with correlation to truth rising from $0.995$ to $0.998$.

On the real panel the instrument is strong: median first-stage $F$ of $5{,}703$,
$95\%$ of products above ten, and two-stage least squares accepted for 49 of 55
products. Impossible positive estimates fall from $5.5\%$ to $0\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}(b)), and the estimates are coherent: branded frozen pizza is
most elastic at $-4.44$ and private-label pretzels least at $-0.11$, neither
pattern imposed.

\subsection{Promotion uplift}
Fig.~\ref{fig:valid}(c) compares estimates against planted truth: mean absolute
error across the three mechanics falls from $0.557$ to $0.010$, a factor of $57$,
and placebo coefficients of $0.001$, $-0.005$ and $0.000$ support parallel trends.
On the real panel the naive comparison again roughly doubles every estimate: the
corrected mailer lift is $0.572$ ($77.1\%$ in units) and the display lift $0.432$
($54.1\%$), each with standard error below $0.005$, with placebo coefficients
$-0.106$, $0.116$ and $-0.066$. The temporary-price-reduction coefficient is
$-0.007$, practically zero --- a consistency check rather than a null result,
since that mechanic is by definition a price cut with no other support.

Combining causal lifts with estimated elasticities charges each promotion for the
margin it gives away. Under the stated cost assumption the display mechanic is
profitable for $60\%$ of products against a median break-even of $72\%$ volume
lift; the price-cut mechanic is profitable for $2\%$, needing $99\%$ lift while
delivering $56\%$. Eight product--mechanic pairs priced below derived cost are
flagged rather than called errors, being consistent with loss leaders whose
basket effects an item-level model cannot see.

\subsection{Forecast accuracy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:dec}(a) reports weighted absolute percentage error on the held-out
weeks: $0.292$ against $0.424$ for the strongest naive baseline, an improvement of
$31.0\%$. Residual bias after smearing is $+2.0\%$ against $-5.1\%$ uncorrected: the
correction slightly worsens WAPE while substantially reducing bias, the right
trade for a forecast feeding an inventory policy where under-prediction becomes
under-ordering. Accuracy tracks volume, from $0.236$ for bag snacks at $18.6$
units per store-week to $0.403$ for oral hygiene at $4.7$. On promoted store-weeks
the forecast is $1.90\times$ the counterfactual baseline, an independent
cross-check on the uplift estimates.

\subsection{Pricing and sensitivity}
The closed form agrees with a brute-force grid search to a maximum relative
difference of $3\times10^{-5}$. Of 54 products, 46 are elastic enough for an
interior optimum; for the other eight the guardrail sets the price, and the
price-move cap binds for $85\%$ of products.

Valuing that recommendation needs care. Price varies within a product across the
held-out weeks because of promotions, so applying an elasticity to a product's
average price and total volume is not the same as applying it row by row; here
the aggregated version understates the gain by about a third. The bias is in the
arithmetic, not the model, so we value every store--week--product row separately
against the $1{,}081{,}938$ profit units actually recorded.

On that basis the recommendation returns $+227{,}003$ units, or $+21.0\%$, and it
does so by selling \emph{less}: units fall $19.7\%$ while margin per unit rises
enough to more than cover the loss, so this retailer was buying volume it was not
profiting from. Because unit cost is assumed, the figure is reported across the
assumptions it rests on: moving elasticity by $\pm 1$ standard error and the
margin assumption by $\pm 5$ percentage points gives $+15.8\%$ to $+26.2\%$. The
direction survives every perturbation, the magnitude does not, so the range is the
result.

\subsection{Does the modelled gain appear in the data?}
The $21.0\%$ was never charged, so we tested it against the panel. The retailer
ran no pricing experiment, but everyday prices for the same product differ across
stores and weeks, and some of that variation lands near our recommendation.
Store--weeks priced within $5\%$ of it were compared against those more than
$15\%$ away, with store--product and week fixed effects absorbed, so a product is
only ever compared with itself in a different week. Run on all held-out weeks this
returns $+741\%$ at $t=129.5$ --- and it is meaningless. Our recommended price
sits above the average shelf price, so ``far from the recommendation'' is close to
a synonym for ``on clearance'': that group
is $69.1\%$ promoted with $16.8\%$ of rows below cost, against $6.2\%$ in the near
group. The comparison recovers the margin gap between full-price and clearance
weeks, which is true by construction.

Excluding promoted weeks removes the confound; both groups then hold no
promotions and no below-cost rows. On the $64{,}485$ rows remaining the estimate is
$+1.6\%$ per store--week (coefficient $0.0156$, standard error $0.0150$, $t=1.0$),
whose $95\%$ interval runs from $-1.4\%$ to $+4.6\%$ and contains zero: the
observational evidence neither confirms nor contradicts the modelled gain.

Table~\ref{tab:ledger} gives that result with every other headline number and its
grade. Both pricing rows are deliberately present: the first alone would overstate
what has been established, the second alone would discard the design work, and the
distance between them is what makes a pilot the next step.

\subsection{Markdown and replenishment}
Fig.~\ref{fig:dec}(c) compares clearance policies over an eight-week horizon with
stock at fourteen weeks of baseline demand. The dynamic programme recovers $6.6\%$
more cash than the fixed $-20\%/-40\%$ ladder and $21.9\%$ more than never marking
down, lifting sell-through from $57\%$ to $91\%$; simulation reproduces its
predicted value to within $0.12\%$.

The optimal ladder runs the opposite way to the textbook one. Averaged over all 54
products, optimal depth \emph{eases} from $20.9\%$ in the first clearance week to
$13.7\%$ in the seventh, rising to $17.8\%$ only in the final week when the
deadline forces the issue (Fig.~\ref{fig:dec}(d)). As stock depletes the urgency to
clear falls, so price can be recovered; a fixed schedule cannot express that, never
observing remaining inventory.

Table~\ref{tab:inv} reports replenishment cost over the held-out weeks across
1{,}324 store--product series. The forecast-driven policy cuts total cost by
$48.1\%$ against the textbook formula while reducing cover from $3.9$ to $2.8$
weeks and raising fill rate from $97.9\%$ to $99.1\%$. Less stock with better
service are usually opposing aims; both improve because the forecast says
\emph{when} cover is needed rather than carrying it all year. The oracle row
bounds what is attainable: perfect foresight would cut cost by $98.3\%$, so the
policy captures $49\%$ of the available saving, which concentrates where demand is
hardest to predict --- $7.6$ per series in the lowest quartile of variability
against $36.1$ in the highest.

\subsection{Limitations}
Unit cost, lead time, holding cost, salvage value and starting inventory appear in
no public retail panel and are stated assumptions; where a conclusion depends
materially on one, it is reported across a range. Estimates rest on observed
rather than randomised variation, so causal claims are conditional on the
fixed-effects specification and are supported, not proved, by the placebo test.
The profit model is item-level and cannot see basket or traffic effects, which is
why below-cost promotions are flagged rather than judged. The replica validates
estimator mechanics under a known process but cannot show that process describes
the real panel, and the observational check has little power, since everyday price
variation is small once promotions are removed.

%=============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

This work presents a retail decision system in which causal estimates of
elasticity and promotion uplift feed pricing, markdown and replenishment modules,
every decision scored on held-out weeks against the heuristic it would replace and
every result published with its grade of evidence. Correcting for price
endogeneity removes all impossible elasticity estimates, correcting for confounded
promotion assignment reduces log-lift error by a factor of $57$ against known
ground truth, and the decision modules beat their incumbent baselines by $6.6\%$
in clearance cash recovery and $48.1\%$ in inventory cost.

The recurring finding is that the correction matters more than the model class: in
each module the largest gain came from removing a bias --- endogenous prices,
confounded promotions, retransformation under a log fit, sunk cost treated as
marginal, aggregation before an elasticity is applied --- rather than from a more
elaborate learner. Evidence grading carries that discipline into the reporting: it
turned a $+741\%$ pricing result into an inconclusive $+1.6\%$, and it is why this
paper recommends a controlled pilot rather than claiming the $21.0\%$ has been
shown. Repricing a random set of stores for one quarter would move the pricing row
from \textsc{modelled} to \textsc{measured}, the only route by which the central
claim can be established.
